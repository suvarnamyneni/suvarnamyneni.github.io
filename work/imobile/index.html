The challenge

A full personal bank, still easy to use.

ICICI Bank set out to revamp iMobile for iOS: an intuitive, delightful personal banking app that holds many functions for people with different financial needs. The interface had to stay simple, accessible and efficient, and still feel engaging.

iMobile launched in 2008 as India's first mobile banking app. The case study describes ICICI Bank as India's largest private bank, with a presence in 16 countries, and iMobile serving over 10 million users. The bank's customers span all age groups, totaling over 100 million.

What we knew before starting

Strongest among Indian apps, still under the global line.

Forrester's analysis showed Indian banking apps performing poorly against international ones. No app passed the global average of 65/100. ICICI received the highest rating among Indian apps, 63/100, and was seen as one of the best among competitors, and the most popular.

Stakeholder interviews covered how the app worked, and where it was limited. Some restrictions, set by the Reserve Bank of India, could not be changed.

Discover & explore

Fifteen interviews, then three personas.

To understand how often people used the app, what hurt, and whether the most-used features could be found, I ran 15 customer interviews. That research became 3 personas for the dashboard redesign.

Competition

Feature-rich, and hard to discover.

No Indian bank app in the set, including HDFC and SBI, stood out for design, features and usability together. The comparison widened to Dhani, Walnut and Paytm. ICICI's app was notable for UPI payments, loan applications and card design. The key functions were still not easy to find. Discoverability and access became the focus.

Heuristic evaluation

The dashboard hid the thing people do most.

A heuristic pass on the dashboard found extra taps, convoluted navigation and long forms. The notes from that review:

  • Stagnant layout Notifications and account updates are not seen on the dashboard.
  • Favourite transaction Not a frequent action, and given too much prominence.
  • Unprioritized grid Many services, not ordered by how the person actually uses them.
  • Profile area Takes 30–35% of the screen that could show relevant data.
  • Balance The most-used action, and not present on the dashboard.
  • Hamburger Hides all actions. No way to discover new features or get personalized suggestions.

Design

Frequent services first. The rest still findable.

The information architecture dropped unnecessary states, rearranged pages, and added Expenditure Analysis, Upcoming Transactions and Discover. Frequent services stay easy to reach. Less common ones stay discoverable. Adding and managing lines of credit, insurance, debit cards and investments was streamlined. Contextual prompts offer a conversation without breaking the flow.

Wireframes

Some simpler journeys fought the business goal.

Concepts covered Login, Dashboard, Accounts, Expenditure Analysis, Fund Transfer, Bill Pay, Offers and Help. The options that most simplified the journey sometimes worked against discoverability and cross-sell. The final choices kept both: a clear path, and a way to meet new features and other financial products.

The dashboard is the gateway. Explorations aimed at an overview of accounts, a way to discover features and products, and quick access to the most-used features.

Solutions

The home is personal from the start.

Top Activities and Discover

The homepage follows what the person actually does. Top Activities keeps frequent tasks close. Discover introduces banking tools and services they have not used yet.

Budgeting and comparisons

Monthly expenses are easier to compare, with visualizations of spending patterns and a place to set a monthly limit.

Expenditure analysis

A category breakdown shows where money went, and alerts fire when a category passes its budget. Vague transaction narration was a pain point, so the narration patterns were tightened to say more clearly what each expense was.

Recurring money

Tell people before the EMI, the bill, or the rent.

Spending patterns were used to spot recurring transactions such as EMIs, bills and rent, and to notify people before those payments. The same narration problem showed up here: descriptions were hard to trust, so the recommendation was clearer narration for recurring expenses.

Transaction history is meant to be easy to read. Transactions are tagged automatically, and people can change or add tags. They can filter and sort in more than one way. That redesign also came from the narration feedback.

Test & evaluate

Test, then fold the notes in before UI.

Wireframes were tested with 8–10 users, then revised before the interface was designed.

Conclusion

Primary tasks stay easy. The rest can still be found.

The case study describes the end product as what the team envisioned: a functional banking app, one of its kind in India. People can do the primary tasks, and the less common features stay discoverable when they need them.

To keep the app relevant, the work also included payments via Siri and QR codes, login via Touch ID, Face ID and voice recognition, and Siri-enabled in-app voice search.

What I learned

Don't get married to an idea.

The project taught the design thinking process, the do's and don'ts of mobile design, especially on iOS, and the importance of not getting married to an idea.
