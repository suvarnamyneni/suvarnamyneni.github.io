The challenge

One deal, five systems, no single source of truth.

Deal Desk, in this case study, is an internal workspace for the transaction analysts who close real-estate deals: they verify numbers, resolve conflicts between systems, issue the Disbursement Authorization that tells a title company who gets paid, and reconcile the money when it arrives.

Before it, that job sat across a task queue, a transaction management system, a vendor and finance system of record, settlement statements and email. The same file also passes through an agent-facing app. Each system owns a slice. None owns the deal. Coordinators, admins and analysts carry the state by hand.

The fragmentation is also about authority. An agent can create a file and upload documents but cannot approve a disbursement. A transaction coordinator prepares almost everything and owns almost no approval. A team lead sees the file and acts on little of it. A broker mostly reviews, and holds the approvals the deal cannot close without. When those distinctions live in five systems, work waits in the gaps.

In scope: intake, checklists, document review, commission and CDA handling, disbursement, settlement reconciliation, corrections and audit history. The agent-facing app and the existing transaction system were mapped as sources and destinations, not redesigned here. Accounting ledgers, payment execution and e-sign engines are out of scope. The workspace hands off to them and records the result.

The lifecycle

States a file can enter, and who may move it.

This comes from the action set in the requirements artifact.

  • Create and intake Deal data arrives from the agent-facing app and the transaction system. Every field carries its source. Agent, TC, admin.
  • Documents and checklist Uploads sit against checklist items, each with a status and comments. Agent, TC, admin.
  • Compliance Mandatory for some roles, optional for others, review-only for the broker. Outcomes land back on the checklist item. TC and admin submit; broker reviews.
  • Commission and CDA Line items, splits, fees and deductions are derived, and editable in one explicit edit mode. Requesting the CDA or DA is separate from approving it. Agent, TC and admin edit; broker approves.
  • E-sign Signature documents are states: requested, out for signature, executed. Agent, TC.
  • After close A post-close submission reopens only the affected step. A change after payment creates a versioned correction, not an edit of history. TC and admin; broker review. Cancel, reopen and escalate are their own states.

Designed behaviour

Six areas, described as the prototype behaves.

The source says these comparisons describe the designed behaviour of the prototype, not a measured before-and-after.

  • Data entry Agents used to retype the same details. In the prototype, fields sync in and each one shows its source system.
  • Financial figures Commission, fees and deductions were calculated in spreadsheets. In the prototype, every figure is derived, with the formula printed under it.
  • Approvals Managers used to sign off line by line over email. Blocking rules run continuously, and only true exceptions surface as alerts.
  • Documents DAs and closing documents were uploaded by hand. The prototype generates them and pushes revisions, with status.
  • Corrections A change creates a versioned correction record and reopens the affected step.
  • Audit trail Instead of reconstructing a trail from inboxes, the prototype captures a field-level change log as it happens, colour-coded by source.

Alerts are meant to be resolved inline, beside the record they refer to. Derived fields are read-only, with the derivation printed underneath. Corrections are new versioned records, not silent edits. Two viewing modes were designed: a guided walkthrough for stakeholders, and an unassisted view.

Named demo scenarios in the prototype include 777 Validation Lane, 888 Testing Lane, 890 Sea Lane, 567 / 678 Creek & River Lane, and 999 Validation Lane. Each one carries a specific hard case: critical alerts, a dual-side deal, an external referral, negative overflow, or a DA that failed to sync.

Requirements I can defend

Five roles. Four permission levels. Approval is not preparation.

With the business analyst, the capability matrix maps every action a file can undergo across agent, admin, transaction coordinator, team lead and broker, marked required, optional, review-only or not permitted. A second view compares the same capability across the current system, the agent-facing app and the new back office.

  1. 01

    Five distinct roles

    The workspace decides what to show from role plus deal state. Evidenced by the artifact.

  2. 02

    Four levels per action

    Required, optional, review-only, not permitted. Review-only is why so much of the workspace is read-only, with one edit mode behind a pencil.

  3. 03

    Approval is separate

    Requesting a CDA and approving it are different actions. Approval controls are absent, not disabled, for everyone except the broker.

  4. 04

    Capabilities carry over

    Each capability was compared across the three systems so an existing role did not quietly lose something it could already do.

  5. 05

    Cancel, reopen, escalate

    These are states, not deletions. History has to survive all three.

Two bets are marked unvalidated in the source. Alerts are the primary surface on the assumption that most analyst time goes to exceptions. Frequency per alert type is unmeasured. Twelve tabs are treated as acceptable if the rail carries the state. That trade-off needs task-based usability testing before it can be called settled.

Reflection

One decision hub per object.

The hardest part of an internal finance tool, as the case study puts it, is not layout. It is deciding where a decision is allowed to be made, and making that place obvious. Revisions moved toward one decision hub per object, with everything else as reference.

Scenarios as data

Replace address-based scenario branching with declarative scenario definitions, so a new edge case is data rather than code.

A shared store

Decompose the workspace by tab on a shared store, and let the workflow rail subscribe to rule state.

Test the alerts

Watch analysts under time pressure. The failure to look for is proceeding past an alert that should have been resolved. Design the manager view earlier: portfolio triage turned out to be a first-class need.

The prototype was built in React, TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind CSS and shadcn/ui.
