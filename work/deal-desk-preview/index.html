Case Study · eXp Realty

Deal Desk

Deal Desk is eXp Realty’s transaction desk, where a real estate deal moves from submitted to paid in one place. I was the UX Designer for the staff and settlement experience: the screens analysts, compliance, and support use to check a deal and pay it out, plus the agent views that show where the deal is. It covers US and Canadian rules.

01

The problem

Getting a deal paid meant jumping between several systems, and the notes between them.

For the analyst who settles the deal, one file meant moving between Task Center, Deal Desk, document AI, and SkySlope. Fee details sat in free-text notes, so staff typed them in again. Once a file said “settled,” an accounting error could stay hidden from the person the agent would call.

For the agent, moving a file forward meant filling fields that did not apply, sometimes with placeholder text, just so the form would accept a document. MLS data did not always come through, so they typed it again. A rejected document did not notify them. Staff could not message them inside the tool, so missing items arrived as a hand-built email. The common question was simple: “Where is my deal?”

In Canada, closing analysts were still sharing spreadsheets with finance.

How might a Transaction Analyst take one deal from submission to the accounting hand-off, with every number traceable and every error visible?

The wider program set out to build an AI-native transaction platform for the US and Canada to replace TRX and SkySlope. In practice the switch was gradual. Leadership chose a phased approach: a new interface, with SkySlope’s document library and APIs kept as the backend for a time.

02

My role and what I owned

UX Designer for staff and settlement on Deal Desk.

A teammate had started an analyst prototype. I extended it instead of starting over, and I owned the flows from the queue through disbursement. My work covered journey mapping, UX design, how AI shows up in the product, and prototypes. Agents work inside the agent platform. Staff work in Deal Desk and Task Center.

Staff and settlement flows

I designed the path from submit to disbursement. I drew the exception paths at the same time as the happy path.

The map the team used

I mapped 19 personas and 14 journeys in Miro, and turned months of demos and meetings into one picture product and operations could point at.

Rules before screens

I wrote the core rules down before drawing anything. Where a rule was not settled yet, I left it open instead of picking one.

The combined prototype

On October 5, 2026, I built a combined Agent and Staff prototype on top of the teammate’s direction: one shared state, and a path a teammate could run.

The agent side

Agent home, inbox, and deal page, on the same visual system as staff, so “where is my deal?” shows the same timeline on both sides.

Checking my own work

I checked the prototype against the journeys and did a role-by-role review. Where the checks disagreed, I logged it.

Discovery: what I watched and read

  • The end-to-end settlement demo in August 2026, where a sale was walked through bonuses, concessions, coordinator fees, the check, dates, accounting, and mentor fees. I treated the open questions in that room as part of the design.
  • About 22 war-room recaps from September 2026, and the schema war room in October 2026.
  • 29 discovery documents and a 345-page program archive.
  • The staff idea marketplace, and agent kickoff notes with a home and inbox spec.

I used Grok to help me work through the archive, the war-room notes, and the journey material, and to pull out rules, conflicts, and open questions. Every rule went back to the original source before it reached a screen.

Mapping: two maps, on purpose

One sheet holds 14 numbered settlement journeys. The other is a wider future-state swimlane for every transaction type, from listing to cancel. They are related but not the same. On any step where the rule, the owner, or the source of truth was still unsettled, I added a flag. Those flags mattered as much as the happy path.

The sources used different names for the same moment: Settle, Mark Disbursed, Disbursed, Settlement Complete, Closed (Paid). I built one status list and a table of the old names, so product, engineering, and operations meant the same thing. With product and operations I agreed five principles: one desk, check before you act, guide the fix, status always visible, and one design system for agents and staff. For every AI or validation check the rule was the same: the system checks, a person decides.

04

Users and personas

Nineteen roles on the map, and five representative personas I composed from it. They are not interview participants, and they are not the fictional people on the prototype screens.

The map has five agent roles and fourteen staff roles, from listing and buyer’s agents to compliance, analysts, Canada’s closing analysts, production support, and post-close. Role names are generalized. I used the set as shared language with product and operations. I also treated the systems as actors: document AI, SkySlope, Task Center, the accounting service, Oracle, and Enterprise. Of the 57 settlement steps, 20 are fully automated, and most failures happen in the hand-offs between those actors.

RH

Remy Holt, Transaction Analyst

US residential settlement.

Goals. Take one file from the queue to a balanced payout without leaving for a check copy or a notes field.

Frustrations. Fees in free-text notes. A file that says settled while an accounting error stays hidden. Critical alerts that block the payout authorization.

Needs. Oldest work first, the next action on each row, compare-and-pick instead of retyping, and a Send button that says why it is disabled.

AC

Avery Cole, buyer’s agent

Submits the deal, then has to live with the status.

Goals. Know where the deal is, and move it without filling fields that do not apply.

Frustrations. SkySlope’s upload flow. Placeholder text just to get a document accepted. Retyping MLS data. No notice when a document is rejected. Missing items sent as a hand-built email.

Needs. A home that shows what needs them first, a commission clearly marked as an estimate, one conversation per deal, and the same status timeline staff see.

NK

Noor Khalil, compliance reviewer

Reviews the file. Does not settle it.

Goals. Approve, send back with a reason, or escalate, without becoming the person who moves the money.

Frustrations. In the combined prototype, compliance actions could be disabled with no reason, so a blocked action looked like a broken button.

Needs. A queue of needs review, escalated, and approved. Settlement is view-only. A send-back returns the file to the analyst, with the reason shown.

SB

Simone Bouchard, closing analyst

Canada. Settles after the analyst prepares the file.

Goals. Close under Canadian practice: a lawyer or conveyancer, firm and closed stages, and a review of overrides.

Frustrations. Spreadsheets shared with finance. US labels swapped onto a Canadian file would be wrong.

Needs. A clear handoff from the analyst, an approve-all path, and Canadian steps that can show before the Canadian accounting connection is ready.

PT

Parker Trent, production support

The person an unpaid agent eventually reaches.

Goals. See an accounting failure before the agent is the first to notice.

Frustrations. A failed payment step after Settle, on a file that still looks finished. Today the error flag is a manual report.

Needs. Automatic error alerts, a support view, clearly different retry and restart actions, and a time-limited flag that clears when someone marks the error handled.

05

Key insights

What the demos, notes, and maps kept telling me.

Most of the work was checking

Most of the effort was comparing dates and amounts between a document and the deal. The actual judgment was the short part.

Handoffs added waiting, not decisions

Work passed from a coordinator to an analyst and got checked twice. The wait did not add a decision.

Status was the loudest pain

15 of 28 active issues in the staff idea marketplace were about this desk. The biggest: a file approved in document AI could still show the wrong status in SkySlope and Task Center.

Exceptions are the job

Mismatches, a failed posting, a changed close date: these were normal, and recovery was improvised each time.

Canada is a different flow

A lawyer or conveyancer, firm and closed stages, and a closing analyst who settles after the analyst prepares. Swapping labels would not be enough.

Alerts had lost their meaning

In the August 2026 settlement demo, the team named 80 critical alerts blocking automatic payout authorizations.

06

Design decisions

The choices that shaped the desk, why I made them, and what they cost.

Decision 1

One file, from the queue to paid

Problem
Analysts still left the desk for a check copy and for notes. A faster screen that sends people somewhere else is still a slow process.
What I chose
One file view from the queue to disbursed, with the legacy accounting and document systems still visible.
Why
Those systems were still in use. Hiding them would have made the status untrue.
Trade-off
A busier screen. I kept it manageable with guided alerts and by showing detail only when it is needed.
Decision 2

Remove the coordinator handoff

Problem
A deal waited between the agent, a coordinator, and the analyst, and was checked twice. The transaction coordinator is hired by the agent and is a payee on the file, not a staff member.
What I chose
On October 5, 2026, I removed the coordinator as a staff role in the prototype. New files go straight to the analyst. The coordinator stays on the file as a contact.
Trade-off
Coordination steps move to the analyst, so the analyst screen carries more.
Decision 3

Structured fee lines instead of notes

Problem
Advances, extended-hand fees, and coordinator fees lived in SkySlope notes. Staff retyped them, and the change log already recorded the review, so the notes were a second, worse copy.
What I chose
Each fee is its own line, with its source shown. Fee rules from the August demo are applied automatically, for example a $100 fee on a non-preferred third-party advance and $50 on extended hand.
Trade-off
Ownership-percentage splits were not confirmed by operations, so I did not design them early. They waited.
Decision 4

When two sources disagree, the analyst picks

Problem
Mismatches between the agent’s entry and the document were retyped by hand, and there was no record of why one number won.
What I chose
A mismatch opens a side-by-side compare: who entered each number, when, and where in the document the extracted value came from. The analyst picks one, commission recalculates, and the choice stays visible and reversible with a timestamp. If unsure, they can ask the agent.
Why
Document AI reads; it does not decide. The audit trail is the point.
Left open
Which source should win by default was not decided, so I left it open instead of guessing.
Decision 5

Blocked buttons say why

Problem
A disabled Send button looked broken. In my role-by-role review of the prototype, a send could also go through with an open underpayment.
Options
Require approval on every file, or only when something triggers it. The August demo moved US approvals to triggers only, such as a company commission change or a personal sale over the threshold. Canada still reviews overrides.
What I chose
Every Send control says why it is disabled: waiting on compliance, a number of blockers still open, or already sent. The header lists what still blocks Send.
Trade-off
A little more friction on the happy path. Trigger-only approvals mean not every US file waits.
Decision 6

“Paid” has to be earned, and it stays final

Problem
A balance gap or an accounting failure could happen after Settle, while the analyst still saw a finished file. That is where the “I haven’t been paid” call starts.
What I recommended
Allow disbursed only when the balance gap is zero, or when a write-off is approved. After that the file is read-only, and a correction is a new post-close record with a reason.
Why
Accounting has already been told, so the history must stay intact.
Trade-off
A correction is extra work, not a quick edit.
Decision 7

Money is an estimate until it settles

Problem
Commission and cap depend on the order deals close in, so an early number can change.
What I chose
Anything shown before settlement is labelled as an estimate, with why it might change and how fresh it is.
Trade-off
The agent home cannot show one confident payout number.
Decision 8

Oldest work first, with the next action on the row

Problem
An ageing file was invisible until someone opened it.
What I chose
A role-based queue: analysts, compliance, and operations each see their own work. Each row shows status, stage, and the next action. Filters follow the lifecycle: new from the agent, needs documents, needs action, ready for the payout authorization, and in settlement.
Still a gap
The journey sheet asked for an explicit oldest-first sort. I logged it as a gap rather than claim it was done.
Decision 9

One layout, two countries

Problem
In Canada the analyst prepares and the closing analyst settles, and overrides need approval. A US screen with new labels would have been wrong.
What I chose
One shared layout, with Canada as its own flow and a real handoff to the closing analyst. Canadian steps can show before the Canadian accounting connection is finished.
Still open
Canada’s approve-everything flow is not in the prototype yet. I held it until the spreadsheet walkthroughs are designed in.
07

The design

The analyst’s path from intake to paid, and the agent’s view of the same deal. These are prototype screens with fictional demo data.

The analyst flow

Step 1 · Checklist

Documents before money

Intake starts with documents. The analyst can ask the agent for anything missing, and that request shows up on the agent’s home. Completing the checklist moves the file toward compliance.

Step 2 · Compliance

Compliance reviews, but does not settle

Compliance sees needs review, escalated, and approved. They approve, send the file back with a reason, or escalate. Settlement is view-only for them.

Step 3 · Alerts

Fix what blocks, ignore what doesn’t

Resolved choices stay on the file. Informational alerts never block. For example, under the $250 minimum the mentor fee is waived, the math is shown, and the alert says Send is not blocked.

Step 4 · Payout package

Build the payout from numbers already on the file

The package confirms payees, roles, amounts, and payment method, and checks that payees add up to the expected funds. Expected funds come from financials, so nothing is entered twice. A changed close date creates a new version, and the history stays on the file.

Step 5 · Settlement

Wait for funds, then mark paid

Settlement adds the payment. A check read by document AI is tagged as extracted, and a row the analyst types is tagged as entered by them. The dates and the balance have to match before Settle unlocks. In the demo, a $500 underpayment is the case that should stop the file from being called balanced.

Step 6 · After payout

Locked, with a way to report problems

Once paid, the file is read-only and leaves the active queue. The analyst can report an issue, which flags it for production support and keeps the file locked. Today that flag is a manual report. The proposal from the demo is an automatic, time-limited flag that support clears when they mark the error handled, so the agent is not the first to notice.

The agent views

The agent desk lives inside the agent platform, on the same visual system as staff. Home shows what needs the agent first, then an estimated commission with why it might change. The inbox is one conversation per deal. A deal can start from a document or from scratch: document AI reads what it can, the agent reviews it, then submits. A missing-document notice must open the filtered checklist; that was a confirmed bug in the old path, and it is a requirement here.

Outcome and where it stands

Designed and prototyped. It has not launched, so there are no production results yet.

The work is still at prototype and demo stage. I am not going to claim a before-and-after on time per deal, screens per settlement, or support contacts. What I can show is the design, the checks I ran on it, and what changed because of them.

I also wrote a Cursor brief to rebuild the prototype as a local app a teammate could clone from GitHub and run, using mock data only. There is no public prototype link.

09

What I learned and what I’d do next

Lessons I’m taking to the next project.

Map the systems, not just the screens

The pain sat in the hand-offs between systems. Those systems had to be actors in the journey, or the screen would hide the failure.

Write the rule before the picture

“It’s an estimate until settled.” “After that, nothing is edited, only added.” “The coordinator is not the analyst.” Those three sentences prevented a whole class of wrong screens.

Design the exceptions early

The happy path is short. Trust comes from the retry, the escalation, the not-paid flag, and the correction that keeps history intact.

A fast prototype can be confidently wrong

AI tools gave me a clickable desk the same day. The journey sheet kept it honest. Placeholder fees from a tool are not a fee schedule.

Leave disagreements visible

Open questions with an owner moved faster than a polished screen that quietly picked a side. Holding back the unconfirmed split was a decision. So was building on a teammate’s prototype.

What I’d do next

  • The real Settle step: a confirm, a payment date, a clear block when close dates disagree, and one name for the end state.
  • Errors after “settled”: an automatic, time-limited flag in Deal Desk and Task Center, visible to support, cleared when someone marks it handled.
  • Approvals and Canada: manager approval on the US triggers, an approve-all path for Canada, and the closing-analyst flow based on the spreadsheet walkthroughs.
  • The two-in-one file: statement and check in one upload, once the rule is decided, plus the workflow guide on the file.

Some questions are still open, and I’d rather leave them open than guess: which source wins when document AI and the deal disagree, whether a combined file is one task or two, how long a post-settlement flag stays up and who clears it, and how an overpayment should work.
