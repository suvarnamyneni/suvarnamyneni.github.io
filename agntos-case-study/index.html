The problem

A paid deal lived in the gaps between tools.

“Where is my deal?”

The question went from the agent to support, to the transaction analyst, to production support, to accounting.

The Transactions Re-imagined program set out to build an AI-native transaction platform for the US and Canada. Before it, taking a deal from submitted to paid meant hopping across separate tools.

  • Agents filled required fields that did not apply to their deal, sometimes typing placeholder text to get past a screen. They re-typed listing data, and a rejected document did not notify them. Their loudest ask was one view of what they needed to do.
  • Transaction analysts settled one deal by moving between the task queue, the desk, the document AI tool, and the document system. Co-agent splits, advances, and coordinator fees arrived as free-text notes and were typed in again.
  • Canada’s closing analysts shared spreadsheets with finance.
  • After “Settled,” an accounting variance or a failed posting could stay invisible to the analyst the agent calls.

How might we carry one deal from submission to the accounting hand-off on one platform, for every role, with every amount traceable and every error visible?

Users and research

The people who touch a deal, and the hand-offs between them.

Agents on a file include the listing agent, the buyer’s agent, a team lead, a team member, and a referral agent. Staff include the transaction coordinator, the transaction analyst, compliance, Canada’s closing analyst, an approver, accounting, production support, broker and operations, and others.

The distinction that shaped the desks: a transaction coordinator is hired by the agent and is paid on the file. A transaction analyst is staff. Those two people are not the same role. Systems count as actors too, and most failures sat in the hand-offs between them.

What I did

  • Read the program archive: discovery documents, war-room notes, the program document, and a settlement demo. I used Grok as a reading aid and checked every rule back to its source.
  • Built an affinity map, the persona set, and a journey sheet. Each step is tagged by actor, system, automation, and build status, and open rules are flagged.
  • Attended war rooms and a workshop, “Documents arrive, I can see my tasks.”
  • Logged open questions, and the places where the sources disagreed, instead of quietly picking a winner.

This pass is synthesis of that archive. I did not run interviews, shadowing, or usability tests with agents or analysts. Timed usability sessions with analysts are the next step.

Key insights

What the archive kept saying.

Checking, not deciding, was the work. Most effort went into comparing dates and amounts between a document and the deal. The judgment itself was the short part. The AI should do the comparing.

Notes were the hidden integration. Advances, fees, and splits lived in free text. Each line needs a source.

“Settled” hid the failure. Errors after settlement were invisible. An exception needs an owner and a queue.

Money is provisional until it settles. Every figure before settlement is labelled an estimate, with the reason it might move.

The US and Canada are different products under one shell. Canada uses a lawyer or conveyancer, firm and closed stages, and a closing analyst. Renaming a US screen would have lied.

Design principles

How AI behaves in a money workflow.

AI reads and checks. A person decides.

Doc AI, the document reader, extracts and flags. Approve, override, and settle are always human actions.

Show the source.

Every value carries a provenance chip: Agent-provided, Extracted, Analyst-entered, or Deal notes.

Admit uncertainty.

States include Processing, Passed, Suggestions, Couldn’t review, and Mismatch. “Passed” and “Approved” are never the same word.

Fail closed, and say how to open.

A critical alert blocks and explains why. An informational alert stays visible and never blocks. Every disabled control carries a reason.

Design the exception first.

A missing signature, an underpayment, or a failed posting is ordinary work. Each gets a queue, an owner, and a reason.

Automate in phases.

First, a person reviews and settles. Later, an AI reviewer comments first. Auto-approval and auto-settle wait until the clear-to-settle documents match and nothing is flagged.

Design decisions

Five choices that make the principle real.

1. Honest AI review states, including “Couldn’t review”

Before. Agents typed placeholder text to get past required fields, and staff re-checked every document by hand. A single “done” state would hide what the AI did not actually check.

Decision. I sketched the AI states before any UI: processing, because Doc AI is asynchronous; high and low confidence; mismatch; couldn’t read; and override. An agent can start a deal from a document. Doc AI fills what it can read. A missing signature is a hard block, cleared by uploading again or by an override recorded against a named person.

The screen stays honest while the model is still working, unsure, or blocked.

2. Review and choose, instead of retyping

Before. When the extracted value and the agent’s value disagreed, the analyst retyped one of them, and the reason was lost.

Decision. A compare view shows both values, who entered each, when, and the document page and section. The analyst picks one. Commission recalculates, and the choice stays visible, reversible, and timestamped. If they are unsure, they ask the agent. Which source wins by default is still an open decision. I left it undecided in the UI rather than inventing a rule.

3. Gates that fail closed, and always say why

Before. When I audited my own combined prototype, sending the disbursement could proceed with an open underpayment, and compliance actions could be disabled with no reason.

The disbursement authorization, the DA on the screens, is the package that says who gets paid. After the audit:

  • Send DA waits on every critical alert.
  • The package will not confirm until the payee total matches expected funds.
  • “Disbursed” requires the money to balance, or an approved write-off.
  • Every disabled Send shows its reason: waiting on compliance, blockers still open, or already sent.
  • An informational alert, such as a mentor fee under the minimum, explains the math and does not block.
  • Compliance is a person in the loop: Approve, Request changes, or Escalate, with a required reason and an audit line.

4. Exceptions become owned tasks, not invisible failures

Before. After “Settled,” a variance or a failed accounting posting could stay invisible, and the agent was the first person to notice.

Decision. Task Center is the spine for what the happy path cannot hold. A missing document, a duplicate, a calculator failure, or a failed hand-off becomes an owned task with a traceable reference. Retry, when the last step failed, and restart, when a middle step failed, look deliberately different. A settled file is append-only, so a correction is its own flow, with a reason. Still proposed, and not treated as finished: a time-boxed flag after settlement that support clears with “manually handled.”

5. One shell, role-aware desks, one shared timeline

Before. The work was spread across many tools. In my own prototype, an agent could land in the staff UI on someone else’s deal, and the coordinator sat on the staff role switcher.

Decision. One shell, many desks. The agent works from Home, Inbox, Transactions, and Doc AI. Staff work the analyst desk, compliance, Canada, and broker and operations. One status chip per file, and the same timeline for the agent and for staff. That is the answer to “where is my deal?” The coordinator came off the staff switcher, and an agent is sent back to their own deal. Queues can be read oldest first, with the next action on each row. In the US, a manager approves an override when a trigger says so. On a Canadian deal, the closing analyst approves the file before it can settle.

AI-native process

How AI shaped the way I worked.

Reading. I used Grok to work through an archive that was already months deep, pulling out rules, conflicts, and open questions. Every rule went back to its source before it reached a screen.

Prototyping. Claude Design carried the visual system and the long canvases. On 5 October 2026 I built a combined Agent and Staff prototype on that visual system: shared state, a role switcher, and both desks. A Cursor and GitHub brief let an engineer clone and run the mock locally. Mock data only, and no secrets.

Guardrail. A fast prototype can be confidently wrong. Figures the model invented were treated as stand-ins, and every build was checked again against the journey sheet. A placeholder was not allowed to become a business rule.

Design system. I own the agntOS design system: tokens, components, keyboard behavior, names for screen readers, and tests. Violet is the AI color. Primary text stays high contrast in light and dark, and the softer hues stay off body copy. A disabled control always says why.

Outcomes

Designed and prototyped. Not yet shipped.

Nothing here is a launch result. Impact will be measured after the product ships. What the prototype shows today:

  • A deal can be followed from the agent’s home to disbursement on one shared timeline.
  • Doc AI shows what it read, what it could not review, and where two sources disagree. A person chooses.
  • Money cannot move while a critical alert is open, and a disabled action says why.
  • A failure after settlement has an owner, instead of disappearing.
  • Auditing my own prototype changed the design. The coordinator left the staff switcher. Send waits on critical alerts. Disbursement waits until the money balances, or a write-off is approved.

After launch I would want to know whether people still have to ask where a deal is, whether errors are caught before money moves, and how often a person overrides the AI. Those answers decide whether automation can go further. None of them are measured yet.

Still open, on purpose

  • Which source wins when Doc AI and the deal disagree.
  • Whether approval is per document or per stage.
  • Whether the US and Canada share a cap.

Reflections

What I would carry into the next one.

  1. Map the systems, not just the screens.

    The failures lived in the hand-offs. A calmer screen on top of a hop is still a hop.

  2. Write the rule before the picture.

    Estimate until paid. Append-only after disbursed. A coordinator is not an analyst. Those rules shaped more of the UI than the visual pass did.

  3. Design the exception first.

    Trust is the retry, the escalation, and the correction that does not rewrite history.

  4. AI earns trust by admitting uncertainty.

    “Couldn’t review” mattered as much as “Passed.”

  5. A fast prototype can be confidently wrong.

    The desks were clickable quickly. The journey sheet kept a placeholder from becoming a business rule.

  6. Sit with analysts before the next visual pass.

    The archive was deep, and it was still secondhand. Timed usability sessions are the work I would start sooner.
